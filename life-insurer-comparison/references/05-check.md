# Step 6: The premium escalation, worked out by the program

**What the program does for every insurer, every build (this is the old "escalation-percentage verification", now a hard gate):**
1. It recomputes every year-on-year percentage from the document's own printed rand figures: (this year minus last year) divided by last year, to two decimals. This is a calculation on sourced figures, not an invented or estimated figure. Where the insurer prints a percentage that is the one shown; the footnote of the letter names, for every insurer, whether its percentages are printed or our own calculation (never a calculated one beside a printed one with no distinction).
2. It checks the printed percentage against the printed rand figures (a difference over 0.06 is flagged).
3. **Anchor check.** The insurer's stated "current premium" and the first row of its own projection table are not automatically the same year; the current premium can match the table's prior year. The program finds the row whose rand figure equals the current premium and flags it if that is not year 1 (or not in the table at all): read the table's year labels literally.
4. **Outliers.** A year that is roughly double the neighbouring years' rate, or a negative figure, is flagged and named in the letter's footnote as worth confirming with the insurer; it is never smoothed away or dropped. A big gap between insurers' averages is flagged for you to look at.
5. **Pattern wording.** If the literal wording contradicts the type you entered (fixed with age wording, or age-rated with only fixed wording) it is flagged.
6. A year with no rand figure shows "not provided" (never blank, never a zero). An insurer with no projection table at all shows "not provided" in every row. The first year has no percentage because there is nothing earlier to compare.

**What you do with the flags.** They go on the confirmation table. When a figure looks unusually high or low (against the other insurers, or on its face) do the research, do not just reassure: re-derive the arithmetic by hand from the document, look for an actuarially plausible mechanism (an age-rated increase stacking multiplicatively with a separately chosen benefit increase; see the hints page) and look for outside corroboration in the insurer's own public guide. Tell Paul honestly what was independently confirmed and what was not. **Keep the client document short:** state the verified percentage as fact, directly; if a genuine outlier needs a client-facing caveat add at most ONE short clause (for example "worth confirming the exact mechanism with [Insurer]"); never a paragraph on the arithmetic or the candidate explanation, and never present a reasoned-but-unconfirmed mechanism as externally verified fact: say less in the document instead.

**The honest answer when Paul asks whether the letter is accurate** is tiered, not blanket reassurance: what is directly sourced and arithmetic-checked; what is reasonable inference applied to a gap (stated as inference); what is sourced externally and should be reconfirmed with the insurer; concrete next steps for any remaining gap. The audit gives the first three counts from the labels each statement carries. Treat the question as an invitation to re-verify.
